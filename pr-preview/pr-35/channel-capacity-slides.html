Channel capacity

Last modified: 2026-10-30 15:41:37 (PDT)

A channel carries symbols from a sender to a receiver, and noise corrupts some of them. This page measures how much information a channel can carry, using the mutual information between what goes in and what comes out.

1 Channels

Definition 1 (Discrete memoryless channel) A discrete channel has a finite input alphabet \(\mathcal{X}\), a finite output alphabet \(\mathcal{Y}\), and, for each input \(x \in \mathcal{X}\), a probability mass function \(p(y \mid x)\) on \(\mathcal{Y}\): the probability that the channel outputs \(y\) when it is sent \(x\). The channel is memoryless if, over \(n\) uses, each output depends only on the input sent at that use:

\[ \mathbb{P}(Y_1 = y_1, \dots, Y_n = y_n \mid X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_n = x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(y_i \mid x_i). \]

This is the setup of Cover and Thomas (2006, chap. 7).

Definition 2 (Binary symmetric channel) The binary symmetric channel with crossover probability \(\epsilon \in [0, 1]\) is the discrete memoryless channel with \(\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}\) that flips its input with probability \(\epsilon\):

\[ p(y \mid x) = \begin{cases} 1 - \epsilon & \text{if } y = x, \\ \epsilon & \text{if } y \ne x. \end{cases} \]

Definition 3 (Binary erasure channel) The binary erasure channel with erasure probability \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) is the discrete memoryless channel with \(\mathcal{X} = \{0, 1\}\) and \(\mathcal{Y} = \{0, \mathrm{e}, 1\}\) that delivers its input with probability \(1 - \alpha\) and otherwise outputs the erasure symbol \(\mathrm{e}\):

\[ p(y \mid x) = \begin{cases} 1 - \alpha & \text{if } y = x, \\ \alpha & \text{if } y = \mathrm{e}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Remark 1 (Erasures are visible). On the binary erasure channel the receiver knows which symbols were lost, but not what they were. On the binary symmetric channel the receiver cannot tell which symbols were flipped.

2 Capacity

Definition 4 (Channel capacity) Send one input \(X\) through a discrete memoryless channel, and let \(Y\) be the output. The joint distribution of \((X, Y)\) is \(\mathbb{P}(X = x, Y = y) = \mathbb{P}(X = x) \, p(y \mid x)\), so the mutual information \(I(X; Y)\) depends on the channel and on the input distribution. The capacity of the channel is the largest mutual information any input distribution achieves:

\[C \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\max_{\mathbb{P}(X = \cdot)} I(X; Y),\]

where the maximum runs over all probability mass functions on \(\mathcal{X}\) (Cover and Thomas 2006, chap. 7). With base-2 logarithms, \(C\) is measured in bits per channel use.

Remark 2 (What capacity measures). \(I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)\) (symmetry of mutual information) is the uncertainty about the input, minus the uncertainty about the input left after seeing the output. So \(I(X; Y)\) is how much one output tells the receiver about the input, on average, and the capacity is that amount when the sender chooses how often to send each input as well as possible.

Definition 5 (Binary entropy function) The binary entropy function is

\[h(\epsilon) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-\epsilon \log_2 \epsilon - (1 - \epsilon) \log_2 (1 - \epsilon)\]

for \(0 < \epsilon < 1\), with \(h(0) = h(1) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}0\).

Remark 3 (Binary entropy is the entropy of a coin). \(h(\epsilon)\) is the entropy, in bits, of a random variable that takes one of two values, one of them with probability \(\epsilon\). It is \(0\) when the outcome is certain and largest, \(1\) bit, at \(\epsilon = 1/2\).

Theorem 1 (Capacity of the binary symmetric channel) The binary symmetric channel with crossover probability \(\epsilon\) has capacity

\[C = 1 - h(\epsilon) \text{ bits per channel use},\]

where \(h\) is the binary entropy function. A uniformly distributed input achieves it.

Proof. Fix any input distribution. By the definition of mutual information,

\[I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X).\]

Given \(X = x\), the output equals \(x\) with probability \(1 - \epsilon\) and differs from it with probability \(\epsilon\), so the entropy of \(Y\) given \(X = x\) is \(h(\epsilon)\) for both inputs. Averaging over \(x\), the conditional entropy is \(H(Y \mid X) = h(\epsilon)\), whatever the input distribution.

\(Y\) takes two values, so \(H(Y) \le \log_2 2 = 1\) bit (Cover and Thomas 2006, sec. 2.6). Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} I(X; Y) &= H(Y) - h(\epsilon) \\ &\le 1 - h(\epsilon). \end{aligned} \]

If \(X\) is uniform on \(\{0, 1\}\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(Y = 1) &= \mathbb{P}(X = 0) \, \epsilon + \mathbb{P}(X = 1) \, (1 - \epsilon) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} \epsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} (1 - \epsilon) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}, \end{aligned} \]

so \(H(Y) = 1\) bit and \(I(X; Y) = 1 - h(\epsilon)\). The bound is attained, so it is the maximum \(C\).

Example 1 (A channel that flips one bit in ten) For a binary symmetric channel with \(\epsilon = 0.1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} h(0.1) &= -0.1 \log_2 0.1 - 0.9 \log_2 0.9 \\ &\approx 0.332 + 0.137 \\ &\approx 0.469 \text{ bits}, \end{aligned} \]

so by Theorem 1 the capacity is

\[C \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits per channel use}.\]

Each use carries one binary digit, but on average only about half a bit of it gets through. At \(\epsilon = 1/2\), \(h(1/2) = 1\) and \(C = 0\): the output is independent of the input. At \(\epsilon = 1\), \(C = 1\) again, since the receiver can flip every bit back.

Exercise 1 (Capacity of the binary erasure channel) Send \(X\) through the binary erasure channel with erasure probability \(\alpha\), where \(0 < \alpha < 1\), and write \(\pi \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{P}(X = 1)\). Let \(E\) be \(1\) if the output \(Y\) is the erasure symbol \(\mathrm{e}\), and \(0\) otherwise. Use base-2 logarithms and the binary entropy function \(h\).

  1. Show that \(H(Y \mid X) = h(\alpha)\).

  2. \(E\) is a function of \(Y\). Use the chain rule for entropy, expanded in both orders, to show that \(H(Y) = H(E) + H(Y \mid E)\).

  3. Show that \(H(Y) = h(\alpha) + (1 - \alpha) \, h(\pi)\).

  4. Conclude that the capacity is \(C = 1 - \alpha\) bits per channel use.

Solution 1.

  1. Given \(X = x\), the output is \(x\) with probability \(1 - \alpha\) and \(\mathrm{e}\) with probability \(\alpha\), so \(H(Y \mid X = x) = h(\alpha)\) for both inputs, and averaging over \(x\) gives \(H(Y \mid X) = h(\alpha)\).

  2. The chain rule gives

    \[H(Y) + H(E \mid Y) = H(Y, E) = H(E) + H(Y \mid E).\]

    Each value \(y\) of \(Y\) fixes \(E\), so \(H(E \mid Y = y) = 0\) for every \(y\), and \(H(E \mid Y) = 0\). Hence \(H(Y) = H(E) + H(Y \mid E)\).

  3. The channel erases with probability \(\alpha\) whatever the input, so \(E\) is independent of \(X\) and \(H(E) = h(\alpha)\). Given \(E = 1\), \(Y = \mathrm{e}\) for certain, so \(H(Y \mid E = 1) = 0\). Given \(E = 0\), \(Y = X\), and since \(E\) is independent of \(X\), \(\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid E = 0) = \mathbb{P}(X = 1) = \pi\), so \(H(Y \mid E = 0) = h(\pi)\). Therefore

    \[ \begin{aligned} H(Y) &= H(E) + \mathbb{P}(E = 1) \, H(Y \mid E = 1) + \mathbb{P}(E = 0) \, H(Y \mid E = 0) \\ &= h(\alpha) + \alpha \cdot 0 + (1 - \alpha) \, h(\pi) \\ &= h(\alpha) + (1 - \alpha) \, h(\pi). \end{aligned} \]

  4. Combining parts 1 and 3,

    \[ \begin{aligned} I(X; Y) &= H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \\ &= h(\alpha) + (1 - \alpha) \, h(\pi) - h(\alpha) \\ &= (1 - \alpha) \, h(\pi). \end{aligned} \]

    Since \(h(\pi) \le 1\), with equality at \(\pi = 1/2\), the maximum over input distributions is \(C = 1 - \alpha\). The receiver loses a fraction \(\alpha\) of the bits and learns the rest exactly; Cover and Thomas (2006, sec. 7.1) reach the same capacity.

3 The noisy-channel coding theorem

Definition 6 (Channel code and rate) An \((M, n)\) channel code for a discrete memoryless channel sends one of \(M\) messages, \(w \in \{1, \dots, M\}\), in \(n\) uses of the channel. It has

  • an encoder, which maps each message \(w\) to a codeword \((x_1(w), \dots, x_n(w))\) of \(n\) inputs, and
  • a decoder, which maps each sequence of \(n\) outputs to a guess at \(w\).

Its rate is

\[R \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\log_2 M}{n} \text{ bits per channel use},\]

and its maximal probability of error \(\lambda^{(n)}\) is the largest, over messages \(w\), of the probability that the decoder’s guess is wrong when \(w\) is sent. These are the definitions of Cover and Thomas (2006, sec. 7.5).

Example 2 (Sending each bit three times) To send one bit over the binary symmetric channel with \(\epsilon = 0.1\), send it three times and decode by majority vote. This is a \((2, 3)\) channel code, with rate \(R = 1/3\). The decoder is wrong when two or three of the copies are flipped, which happens with probability

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda^{(3)} &= 3 \epsilon^2 (1 - \epsilon) + \epsilon^3 \\ &= 3 (0.01)(0.9) + 0.001 \\ &= 0.028, \end{aligned} \]

down from \(0.1\) for sending the bit once. Repeating more often lowers the error further, but the rate falls toward \(0\) as it does. MacKay (2003, chap. 1) works through this code.

Theorem 2 (Noisy-channel coding theorem) Let \(C\) be the capacity of a discrete memoryless channel.

  1. For every rate \(R < C\) there is a sequence of \((\lceil 2^{nR} \rceil, n)\) channel codes, for \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), whose maximal probability of error \(\lambda^{(n)}\) tends to \(0\).

  2. Conversely, any sequence of \((\lceil 2^{nR} \rceil, n)\) codes with \(\lambda^{(n)} \to 0\) has \(R \le C\).

Remark 4 (Reliable communication at a positive rate). Example 2 buys a lower error rate with a lower rate, and repetition alone drives the error to \(0\) only as the rate falls to \(0\). Theorem 2 says that this trade-off is not forced: over the channel of Example 1, long enough codes send about \(0.53\) bits per channel use with an error probability as small as we like. Above capacity, no code can.

Shannon (1948) stated the theorem and sketched its proof. Cover and Thomas (2006, sec. 7.7) prove part 1 by choosing codewords at random, and Cover and Thomas (2006, sec. 7.9) prove part 2 with Fano’s inequality. MacKay (2003, chap. 9 and 10) gives another account.
